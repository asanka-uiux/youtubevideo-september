The build story

Not boring.
Still capable.

Custom Offsets Build Team . TK_DATE . 4 Min Read

You bought a heavy duty truck to do heavy duty work. Then you look at it for a while and it's just kind of boring. That's exactly where we were with this 2026 Ram 3500.

So this build asks one question: what can you add, and what should you skip, to keep a work truck working while it looks good doing it? Every part got called a show mod, a work mod, or both, and the meter at the top of this page kept score the whole way.

We build one of these every year, and we always drift too far toward show. It's easy to overdo it when all the fun parts are sitting right there. This time, the goal was to land in the middle.

Wheels and tires, the decision that limits everything else

The 26x14s are the widest we can go without losing towing ability, which makes them the one decision that limits everything else. Honestly, they're almost too wide. You can go wider on a 4in lift if you want the look, but if you haul heavy, go narrower.

A 14in wide wheel changes more than the look. It moves the contact patch outboard, and the bearings, steering links, and track bar all feel that on every mile. That's why 26x14 was our ceiling, not our starting point, and it's why three of the next four parts exist to hold the front end straight underneath it.

The parts that earn their keep

Four groups do the real work, and only one of them is visible with the truck sitting still.

The Kryptonite steering group is the one nobody sees and the one this truck needed most. Solid 7075 links and a dual stabilizer on two Fox 2.0 shocks, all hidden behind the wheels, all there because a 14in tire puts more leverage on the steering than Ram planned for.

The BDS track bar goes on for the same reason: lift a solid axle and it stops sitting centered under the truck. An adjustable bar puts it back.

The differential cover is the quiet one. Gear oil is the first fluid to give up when you drag a gooseneck up a grade in July, and the finned Banks cover keeps that oil cooler instead of asking you to carry more of it. Drain, clean, bolt on, refill. It's an afternoon.

The brakes are the least exciting parts on the truck and the first ones you'd miss. Drilled and slotted rotors, black so they don't look rusty behind an open spoke wheel, on a truck whose tires got heavier while the trailer stayed just as heavy.

The parts that are just for you

The glow kit is the purest show part here. Rock lights under the body, ring lights in the wheels, an afternoon on a creeper running wire, and none of it makes the truck work better. Park it after dark at a show and it's the first thing people walk over to.

The cab and mirror lights are all about the look. They trace the roofline and the sides of the truck so it reads as one finished build instead of a truck with some lights added on.

The billet diesel and DEF caps are the cheapest change on the whole build and the one only you'll ever see. Nobody opens your fuel door but you.

And the exhaust. A 5in DPF back in black that leaves every piece of emissions equipment on the truck. It looks like a work part and scores like a show part, and we called it that way.

Where it landed

Dead center, for the first time ever.

Nine parts averaged out, and the marker sits right on the middle. The glow kit pulled hard toward show, the steering group pulled just as hard toward work, and the two mostly cancel out.

That's the story of the build. The truck spent its summer parked on grass at shows and hooked to a gooseneck the rest of the week.

If your truck still works for a living, don't leave a part off. Come down a number instead. Go narrower than 14in. Everything else on this list either helps the truck do its job or costs nothing but money.

That's the whole question this build exists to answer. The rule was simple: every part gets called a show mod, a work mod, or both, and we track the running average instead of guessing at the end. Land in the middle and you've got a truck that turns heads at a show and still hooks up to a trailer on Monday. This one landed dead center, and the real proof is that the truck spent its whole summer doing both jobs.

On this truck, 14in wide is the line. It's the widest we could go without losing towing ability, and honestly, it's almost too wide.

A work mod changes what the truck can do: runs cooler, stops better, steers straighter under load, keeps the bed dry. A show mod changes how it looks and nothing else. Plenty of parts are both, which is why the meter is a scale and not a switch. Watch out for the parts that look great and quietly cost you capability.

Three. The wheels are the big one: 26x14 is as wide as this truck goes without giving up towing, so if yours works every week, go with a narrower set. Drilled rotors are the wrong call if you tow at the top of the rating, since drilling adds stress risers and heat cycling is what kills rotors behind a gooseneck. And the glow kit is the easy skip. It costs you nothing in capability. Everything else here either helps the truck work or doesn't sacrifice anything for performance.

Dead center. The glow kit pulled it furthest toward show, the Kryptonite steering group pulled it furthest toward work, and the two nearly cancel out.

The lift, track bar, steering group, diff cover, and exhaust are all cut for this chassis and axle, so a Ford or GM owner needs the equivalent, not the same box. What transfers is the order of operations: pick the lift that fits your tire instead of the tallest one on the shelf, spend on steering and cooling before lighting, and leave the emissions equipment alone.
